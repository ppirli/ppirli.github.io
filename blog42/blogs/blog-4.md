# 42 Books / 42 Years Blog Post 4: Topic Modeling
This is the fourth and final post in a series of blogs that use digital humanities methodologies to examine the books comprising the [History of Black Writing (HBW)](https://hbw.iu.edu)'s 2025 exhibit [*42 Books / 42 Years*](https://hbw.iu.edu/news-events/events/42Books-42Years/index.html). This post deviates from the stylometric methodology of the first three blog posts, and puts focus on the predominant topics in the *42 Books / 42 Years* corpus, using topic modeling.

## Introduction
In the first three blog posts, I had undertaken a stylometric analysis of our corpus of 42 books. In [the second post](./blog-2.html), I landed on the topic of embodiment as manifesting in the linguistic style of the novel. In [the third post](./blog-3.html), my analysis of embodiment in style branched out into the questions of narrative voice, temporal perspective in narration, and non-human embodiment.

I have been, then, thinking about topics, matters, questions—things that appear in the novel. An evident continuation of this line of thought is to look directly at the words that co-occur within and among the textual narratives that make up our corpus. In so doing, I can examine the discursive contours of our corpus of 42 novels.

## Methodology
But, how? The answer to this question comes by way of topic modeling, a longstanding tool of computational literary analysis. In short, topic modeling takes a set of documents, looks at words that co-occur in different documents, and assigns words into topics based on their probability of co-occurrence.

### On Preprocessing
Applied to novels, topic modeling promises to highlight the salient discourse(s) that characterize a given narrative. But, how to apply this method to a corpus of novels? The answer to this question mainly lies in preprocessing. Carson Sievert and Kenneth Shirley,[^1] in their conference paper introducing the LDAvis topic model visualization tool, use a corpus of posts from discussion forums. Such posts are short, and, being actual instances of communication, often have a clear topic. Novels are not so—they are documents of extensive length, with multifarious themes, characters, and plotlines. If I feed the topic model with the 42 novels as uniform documents, all I will get is very general topics; i.e. ubiquituous matters that occur in almost every novel and don't really say anything special about the novels. Thus, one preprocessing step I took was the segmentation of each novel into chunks of 500 words. In this way, instead of 42 documents that may offer only general themes, I have 1,284 documents that yield meaningful thoroughlines occurring among the 42 novels. Chunking also allows me to track the narrative trajectory of each novel by examining which topic is more prevalent in which chunk(s).

Prior to chunking, I part-of-speech-tagged the corpus (again), and excised any word that wasn't a noun, and then lemmatized these nouns. My rationale is that nouns are the most likely part of speech (POS) to throw the thematic elements of novels into relief. Closed class POSs like determiners and pronouns (so called because they are of a limited number, and new ones cannot be easily created in a language) are bound to say more about the style of a novel rather than about its content. In the case of open class POSs, proper nouns will inevitably create topics that exclusively pertain to one novel; adjectives and adverbs are primarily descriptive, and unlikely to yield much in the way of thematic information; verbs are, admittedly, likely to produce this kind of information, but since I already engaged in an analysis of action verbs in a previous post, I opted to not include them in this analysis. Finally, I lemmatized the nouns, as such a difference as that between "apple" and "apples" does not create any sort of meaningful thematic difference.

Aside from filtering for nouns alone, I also added two conditions to my preprocessing pipeline: (1) I excluded any noun that appeared in less than 4 chunks; (2) I did the same for any noun that appears in over 80 percent of the chunks. The first condition will remove any overly specific terms from the corpus, while giving space to words that, while perhaps specific to a novel, take up a considerable part of said novel. The second condition removes any overly general terms that will not yield and specific thematic information from the corpus.

The second condition also helps with the imbalanced number of chunks produced by each novel across the corpus. The imbalance is greatly caused by Leon Forrest's *Divine Days* (1992), which produces a whopping 193 chunks compared to the corpus's mean of around 30 chunks. Forrest's gargantuan novel is an outlier in the corpus, and as [Table 1](#Table1) shows, removing *Divine Days* from the corpus decreases the standard deviation of the dataset considerably. Removing Forrest's novel also makes the dataset less skewed (as evidenced by a increased proximity between the mean and the median). Less skewness in the dataset means that the data is more symmetrical, which in turn provides proof that *Divine Days* is an outlier. Nevertheless, removing *Divine Days* from the corpus altogether would ruin this series of posts' focus on the 42 novels in the *42 Books / 42 Years* corpus. I could alternatively pick only a random number of chunks from *Divine Days* that is close to the mean, but that would most likely mean losing important information residing in the left out chunks. In comparison to these options, making it so that words that appear in above 80% of the chunks makes *Divine Days*'s chunks less likely to influence the dataset on the whole.

Another, admittedly more important, matter that makes the imbalance created by Forrest's novel less significant lies in a preprocessing step I didn't take. It is common in topic modeling applications to limit the vocabulary of the topic model to the *n* most frequent words in the corpus (usually *n* is around 5,000). By skipping this step, I make it so that chunks from other novel's don't have to fight with *Divine Days*'s chunks in order to be selected in the model's vocabulary. The issue of very specific words is virtually resolved by the exclusion of proper nouns and any noun that appears in fewer than 4 chunks, anyhow.

### On What Comes After Preprocessing
What comes after this rather boring and convoluted preprocessing step is rather simple: I use Latent Dirichlet Allocation (LDA) to create a topic model with 10 topics.[^2] Once the topic model is created, I use three visualization methods:

1. **PyLDAvis**: PyLDAvis—the Python port of LDAvis mentioned above—allows me to see the most salient words in the topic model, as well as words that are the most relevant to each topic. A word's salience is computed according to a formula that checks its frequency across the corpus as well as its distinctiveness across topics. In other words, high overall frequency + high specificity = high salience. Relevance is computed according to a formula that looks for either words that are the most representative for that topic. The relevance metric is affected by the lambda (λ) parameter, which can be between 0 and 1: a lower λ value looks for words exclusive to a given topic, whereas a higher λ value looks for words that occur most frequently in that topic. The λ value of 0.6 is often used as a sweet spot for purposes of the human interpretability of the topic, as identified by Sievert and Shirley[^3]. Following this, I take 0.6 as the default λ value. Aside from providing the most salient and relevant words, pyLDAvis also visualizes the distance between topics (based on the co-occurrence of words among topics) on a two-dimensional plot.
2. **Principal Component Analysis (PCA) on Topic Prevalence Values**: PyLDAvis is limited by the fact that it gives exclusive focus to topics, and overlooks the documents that underlie these topics. Accordingly, it gives us no information on which topic defines which novel(s). So as to resolve this problem, I took the means of the prevalence of each topic in every chunk in a given novel, for all 42 novels. This gave me the percentage of each topic's prevalence in each novel. I then put each novel, as represented by ten topic prevalence values, on a two-dimensional PCA plot. In addition to the 42 novels, I also placed the loadings for each topic on the plot. The loading of a given topic displays to what direction that topic pulls the novels which center on it.
3. **Heatmaps**: Using the topic prevalence values for each chunk, I produce 42 heatmaps that display the narrative trajectory of each novel according to the movement among topics. These heatmaps can also tell us about how broad the variance of topics in each novel is—some novels are bound to be rather one-note, whereas others will show more variance across topics.

## Analysis
### PyLDAvis Dashboard: What Can 10 Topics Tell Us About 42 Novels?
My plan for this blog post is that its analysis will be less focused on certain novels, as the two previous posts have been. The pyLDAvis dashboard accessible by way of [this link](./pyldavis-dashboard.html)[^4] provides good ground to begin a post with such a general approach. The dashboard displays the 10 topics in varying levels of distinctiveness; the most distinctive topic on the Y (PC2) axis appears to be topic 10. The top ten words most relevant to this topic (visible when the topic bubble is hovered over or clicked) are "phone," "kid," "book," "mom," "guy," "beach," "bag," "stuff," "job," and "lot." These words pertain to a mix of topics, including modern concerns ("phone," "job"), family ("kid," "mom"); there is, less conclusively, also a set of words that may appear in conversations between women ("guy," "bag"). Topic 3, the most similar topic to topic 10, continues the discourse of modernity ("car," "door," "street," "truck") with an added note of words that seem to belong in a work of noir fiction ("gun", "cop"). Likewise, topic 9, the next topic most similar to topic 10, shares the discourse of family ("mother," "father"), and adds to it the discourse of domesticity ("house," "room," "kitchen," "table").

On the bottom-right quadrant of the plot, topics 4, 5, and 1 form a cluster. Topic 4 unambiguously pertains to antebellum racial injustice ("race," "slave," "master," "white," "slavery," "negro"). Accordingly, it is likely to be the primary topic of 19th century black novels like William Wells Brown's *Clotel* (1853), as well as later books that center on 19th century racial injustice, like John Oliver Killens's *Great Gittin' Up Morning* (1972). At first glance, topic 5 seems to have little similarity to topic 4; it is primarily concerned with internal being and morale ("soul," "spirit," "power") and orality ("voice," "story"). But, if you click on topic 5's bubble and hover over "soul," "spirit," "power," and "order"—doing so allows you to see the percentage distribution of a word across topics—you can see that topic 4 assigns these words a relatively high probability. The same could be said of topic 1, as regards "power" and "order." This suggests the existence of a subtheme among these three topics that are distinct in their primary themes. This suggests that the discourses of internality, orality, and racial injustice in some of the 42 novels tend to run parallel to a discussion of authority and power. We can add to this observation topic 1's primary themes of family (child, wife, son, father, mother, daughter, family, boy) and education (teacher, school). The discourses of internal character, racial injustice, family, and authority are easy to link to the early Black novel: in Paul Laurence Dunbar's *The Sport of the Gods* (1902), a Black family suffers the dissolution of their very sense of being due to a false allegation of theft levied by a powerful white man; in Frank J. Webb's *The Garies and Their Friends* (1855), one interracial and one Black family go through, and attempt to overcome, a series of misfortunes caused by racial prejudice. The theme of education is important in Frances E. W. Harper's *Iola Leroy* (1892), in which the protagonist works as a teacher, and in Sutton E. Griggs's *Imperium in Imperio* (1899), which opens with the employment of a racist white teacher at a school for Black children. Orality is more difficult to pin onto the early Black novel, as nineteenth- and early-twentieth-century Black thinkers exalted literacy and the written word as a tool of power and liberation. It may be that out of the two discourses of topic 5 identified above, it is the discourse of spirituality, character, and morale is what motivates the early Black novel, rather than orality, which appears on other novels. Or, it could be the case that an author like Killens, who writes of the nineteenth century in the post-literate latter half of the twentieth century, makes use of orality in his novel without any ideological scruples.

Note that, when you click on topic 1 and hover over such words as "child," "wife," "son," "father," "mother," the size of topic 9 either stays the same or increases, whereas topic 10 more or less disappears. Topics 1, 9, and 10 all deal with the discourses of domesticity and family; one thing that differentiates topic 10 from the former two is its use of shorter, perhaps more modern, words like "kid," "mom," and "boy." This suggests that there is a chronological element that separates these topics, and that a higher occurrence of topic 10 in a given novel is an indicator that that novel has a recent date of publication.

Topics 6, 2, and 7 seem to form another cluster on the bottom-left quadrant of the intertopic distance map. Topic 6, the largest topic out of the ten, appears to primarily concern the human body ("body," "hair," "arm," "mouth," "blood," "finger," "voice," "lip," among others). Specifically, certain words ("girl," "mother," "breast," "dress") suggest that this topic's primary discourse pertains to the female body, and it could be said that such words as "hair" and "lip" are associated more with femininity than masculinity. The undertones of sensuous romance ("blood," "finger," "lip," "shoulder," "pain") and motherhood/girlhood ("girl," "mother," "baby," "child," "doll") should also not be ignored. All in all, then, topic 6 stands as a nexus of topics that relate to femininity. It is altogether not very surprising this topic exists and occupies so much of the 42 novels—after all, 19 of the 42 authors in our corpus are women, and it's illogical to suggest that male authors don't write about women. What is perhaps surprising is the state of topic 2 as the male counterpart to topic 6. "Boy" stands as the fourth most relevant word for the topic, and is surrounded by words that primarly relate to adventure and the outside world ("light," "wind," "sea," "sun," "air," "land," "world", "stone," "tree"). Words pertaining to urban life ("landlord," "street," "crowd," "city") underline this connection between male embodiment and externality. This connection contrasts with Topic 6's focus on corporality and sensuality. The divide is reminiscent of our first blog's [discussion of a gender divide](./blog-1.html#a-gender-divide-light-darkness-the-city-and-style) that hinged on the binary of internality and externality between the female- and male-authored halves of our corpus. That being said, the word "mother" is also very relevant to topic 2, and could suggest that I am misreading the topic altogether. More research is needed, as they say; such research may be found in [an upcoming section](#heatmaps-individual-close-ups).

Touching both topics 6 and 2, topic 7 seems to be interested in a very different kind of embodiment. "Slave" appears as the key word that relates to the body; this word is surrounded by words that primarily depict the environment of the slave ("field," "road," "horse," "house," "river," "land," "plantation," "crop," "cotton"). The words "war" and "soldier" suggest the unrest of the Civil War. If you hover over such words as "slave," "war," and "plantation," you will see that topic 4 is deeply linked to topic 7 in its discussion of slave life. And yet, they are far apart from each other on the map, at least on the X axis. This distance likely stems from the fact that topic 4 is interested more in the social and political dimensions of being enslaved and/or Black ("country," "freedom," "law," "education," "crime"), whereas topic 7 is interested in the bodily experience of being enslaved. It is intriguing that the topic model put these two matters in separate topics. It could be that they occur in different novels, for some reason. Or, maybe they co-occur in different parts of the same novel. They could even co-occur in same parts of a novel, but their different levels of occurrence in different novels could warrant their being separated.

You might have noticed the existence of "gwine" in topic 7. This word is an interesting inclusion, as it is not a noun at all; it's the present participle of "go" in early Black vernacular. In other words, it's the early Black counterpart to standard written English's "going." "Going," of course, doesn't appear in any topic whatsoever; it's been correctly parsed as a verb by the POS-tagger. The inclusion of "gwine," which is bound to occur in a novel depicting early Black American life, in topic 7 says something about the computational methods I use. Earlier, I had mentioned that a POS-tagger, being a machine learning model, is trained on annotated corpora of English-language text. "White" dialects of English provide the "norm" to which ethnic English varieties are the periphery in many different aspects of Anglophone existence; the field of machine learning is no exception. This fact has the unfortunate effect of making such machine learning models as POS-taggers potentially work suboptimally when used on Black and other non-white texts due to such texts' use of non-white English.

Finally, topic 8 stands as the smallest topic on the map; it also keeps its distance from the rest of the topics on the bottom-left quadrant. A closer look at the most relevant words reveals why topic 8 is unique: the words "douen," "allyou," "eshu", "hinte," and "tallpeople" occur exclusively in Nalo Hopkinson's *Midnight Robber* (2000); additionally, while "pickney" occurs in both *Midnight Robber* and Claude McKay's *Banjo* (1929), it occurs in the former novel over 100 times, while occurring in the latter novel only 3 times. "Pickney" is an informal word for "child" used in Caribbean varieties of English; similarly, "douen" is a spirit entity in Trinidadian folklore. "Allyou" is the plural of "you" in Trinidadian Creole—again, the confusion of the POS-tagger when faced with non-white English rears its head. "Eshu" is the deity of messengers and language in West African Yoruba religion. One may assume that Eshu should be a proper noun, but "eshu" is actually an analogue of the Internet in Toussaint, the Caribbean-inspired planet where *Midnight Robber* is set. "Hinte" and "tallpeople" are Hopkinson's inventions; the former refers to female douens, while the latter is used by Toussaint natives to refer to human settlers. Topic 8 is interesting in that it signals the uniqueness of *Midnight Robber*'s setting compared to the other 41 novels. At the same time, topic 8 comes with the disadvantage of not saying much about any novel other than that of Hopkinson's.

Now that we have delved into the predominant topics in our corpus, it's time to see what topics dominate what novels, as well as what topics are the most unique in terms of their belonging to particular groups of topics. In order to do that, let's use PCA again.

Immediately, the loadings for topics 3 and 4 jump out as the two nodes keeping their distance from the center. Topic 4 paints a picture not too dissimilar to [the first blog's analysis of chronology in the 42 novels](./blog-1.html#chrono-stylometric-drift-unconscious-style-or-something-else), where, in analyses that looked at most frequent words in the corpus, earlier Black novels had clustered on the center-right of the PCA plot. Since this clustering occurred in analyses that looked at both low and high numbers of most frequent words, I had surmised that early Black novels had clustered together due to similarities in both content and style. I had also inferred that the 1972 novel *Great Gittin' Up Morning* was included in this cluster due to not only its thematic similarity to early Black novels, but also its use of 19th-century historical sources, which potentially could lead to an unconscious stylistic change in Killens's writing.

In the purely content-based analysis of this post, we see this cluster to be replicated. Joel Augustus Rogers's *From "Superman" to Man* (Rogers_Superman_1917) deviates from the center the most on the X axis, just as it did on the PCA plots of the first blog. *Clotel* (Brown_Clotel_1853), *Imperium in Imperio* (Griggs_Imperium_1899), *Great Gittin' Up Morning* (Killens_Morning_1972), and *Iola Leroy* (Harper_Iola_1892) closely follow. This is perhaps a result we had understood already in the previous posts; early Black novels concern themselves more with the discourses of slavery and racial violence than anything else.

It is interesting, however, to note that three early Black novels—*Sport of the Gods* (Dunbar_Sport_1902), Nella Larsen's *Quicksand* (Larsen_Quicksand_1928), and *The Garies and their Friends* (Webb_Garies_1857)—are situated farther from topic 4, and closer to topic 9, though they are still to the right of the center cluster due to topic 4's influence. Topic 9 primarily deals in the discourses of family and domesticity, which all three of these novels put emphases on. I had discussed the importance of family in Dunbar's and Webb's novels in [the previous section](#pyldavis-dashboard-what-can-10-topics-tell-us-about-42-novels). *Quicksand* is perhaps less about a family as such, but it details how the independent, well-educated Helga Crane is transformed—hollowed out—by the trauma of repeated childbirth as the wife of a reverend. These three novels' unique positioning show us how they present an intersection of distinct discourses.

I can infer that it is topic 9 that moves these three novels to the left for two reasons: (1), because they move not just to the left, but also a little downwards, where topic 9 is; (2), because I read Dunbar's and Webb's novels (I only skimmed through *Quicksand* 😛). Such an inference cannot be easily made with the four novels pulled away from the center by topic 3—namely, Chester Himes's *Cotton Comes to Harlem* (Himes_Cotton_1965), Ernest J. Gaines's *A Lesson Before Dying* (Gaines_Lesson_1993), Richard Wright's *Native Son* (Wright_Native_1940), and Gordon Parks's *The Learning Tree* (Parks_Learning_1963). It cannot, because the loadings for most of the topics that can pull these novels down are too close to one another to have a distinctive pull on the X axis. The exception to this observation is *Native Son*, whose rightward trajectory suggests that topic 4 is partially prevalent in this novel. This suggestion is to be confirmed (or denied) in the more close-up analysis of [the next section](#).

It is noteworthy that, with the exception of Gaines's 1993 novel, these novels that are characterized by their centering on topic 3, which evokes an urban form of violence, have been published in the mid-nineteenth century. There is, here, a chronological shift of content: the nineteenth-century concerns of slavery and lynching, which have a Southern connotation, are replaced with the concern of urban violence with the twentieth-century event of the Great Migration, which saw large swathes of Black people move from the rural South to Northern cities. Wright's and Himes's novels take place in the big Northern cities of Chicago and New York, respectively, which illuminates a direct link between urban violence and the Great Migration. Parks's semi-autobiographical novel takes place in rural Kansas, but it must be underlined that Parks was a descendant of the "Exodusters," who migrated from Mississippi to Kansas in the late-nineteenth century, and who hence figured as a precursor to twentieth-century Black migrants to the North. It could be surmised that these northward migrants and the Exodusters had similar concerns with regard to their new location. The case of Gaines's novel is more complicated, as it takes place in Louisiana. Nevertheless, the overarching plot element of the novel is a grave criminal act—the murder of three men in a shooting—falsely levied against a Black man. So, even though this novel takes place in the South, there is the overlapping discourse of crime and violence.

On the bottom-right corner, the loadings for topics 6 and 10 figure as distant to the aggregation around the center, though not as much as those for topics 3 and 4. The topic 10 loading causes Jasmine Guillory's *By the Book* (Guillory_Book_2022) and Carolyn Tillman's *Life on Wheels* (Tillman_Wheels_1975) to noticeably trail downwards. Mutual threads of internality, emotionality, and sentiment have been discussed in the two previous posts; it appears that this mutuality contunies via the discourses of modernity and family. Topic 6, associated with corporality and sensuality, has the constellation of Eric Jerome Dickey's *Sister, Sister* (Dickey_Sister_1996), Percival Everett's *Zulus* (Everett_Zulus_1990), and Ntozake Shange's *Sassafrass, Cypress & Indigo* (Shange_Sassafrass_1982) veering toward it, and away from the center. Interestingly, both Jerome's and Shange's novels revolve around three sisters pursuit of love. *Zulus*, on the other hand, concerns Alice Achitophel, who is the only woman capable of childbearing in a dystopian, post-apocalyptic world where every woman undergoes forced sterilization. Alice is also morbidly obese, and this intersection of the marginality of the fat female body and its forbidden capability of procreation entails a substantial focus on corporality.

In truth, though, all novels that are situated below and to the left of topics 5 and 8 are steered, in one way or the other, by the loadings of topics 6 and 10. All but *Zulus*, Leon Forrest's *Divine Days* (Forrest_Days_1992), William Demby's *The Catacombs* (Demby_Catacombs_1965), Victor LaValle's *The Changeling* (LaValle_Changeling_1997), and *Sister, Sister* are written by women; this makes a ratio of 12 female authors to 5 male authors. This gender disparity among novels in which topics 6 and 10 are dominant could suggest that the female authors selected for inclusion in our corpus are more likely to discuss the matters of sensuality and corporality than the male authors.

This leaves us with the loadings of topics 1, 2, 7, 5, and 8, which are too close to the zero-line of both primary components (PCs) for us to make any meaningful inference about their influence on the plot. That the loadings of these topics are too close to the center does not necessarily mean that their predominance in a given novel does not make that novel unique. For example, as we have seen earlier, topic 8 is unique to the utmost; it embodies the Caribbeans-inspired setting of *Midnight Runner*.

So, what's going on? The answer to this question is connected to a downside of PCA, or any other method of dimensionality reduction. In order to convert a ten-dimensional dataset into two, the algorithm needs to identify which variables are the *most* informative about the variation observed in the dataset. To do that, the algorithm needs to sacrifice some information. While topic 8 certainly presents a variation between *Midnight Runner* and the rest of the corpus, it doesn't actually say all that much about any of the other novels that make up the rest of the corpus. In contrast, topics like 3, 4, 6, and 10 explain variance in a perceptible number of novels, forming these novels into clusters.

There is no easy solution to this downside of dimensionality reduction. I could place a three-dimensional PCA plot here; it would explain 53.5% of the variance in the dataset, compared to the two-dimensional plot's explaining 39.7% of it. Anything more than that could not be visualized easily—even the three-dimensional plot would be rather hard to parse, requiring the user to rotate the plot from one position to another in order to interpret what's going on. Instead of making you go through that, let's look at the narrative trajectories of individual novels in terms of topic prevalence using heatmaps.

What I will do is to look at some novels steered upward by topic 3, some steered rightward by 4, and some surrounded by topics 1, 2, 5, 7, and 8. In so doing, I will be able to get some insight into the nuances of our novels' positionings on [Figure 1](#Figure1).

Of the four novels moving toward topic 3 discussed in [the previous section](#pca-plots-the-clustering-of-discourses), the urban crime novel *Cotton Comes to Harlem* veered the closest to the topic. Accordingly, we see in *Cotton*'s heatmap an almost exclusive presence of the third topic. In the middle of the novel, topic 6 seems to shine through, suggesting the presence of eroticism in the novel (maybe mixed in with violence, as "blood" is one of the most relevant words to the topic). Such topics as topic 6 foreground discourses that produce literary texts; of course, authors produce what they write, but their writing is informed by certain ways in which we see and understand things. In a large enough corpus, the same goes also for topic 3. But, in our small corpus that does not have a huge presence of crime fiction novels like *Cotton*, it can be said that Himes's novel produces topic 3's discourse for the corpus. This is similar to the case of topic 8 and *Midnight Robber*, the difference being that enough novels share topic 3's discourse for it to have a distinctive presence in [Figure 1](#Figure1).

One of the novels that share topic 3's discourse of urban violence is *Native Son*, wherein Wright holds the systemic causation of the crimes committed by his protagonist, Bigger Thomas, under the microscope. I'll readily admit that I have not read all of the 42 novels I am responsible for conducting a computational analysis of. However, *Native Son* is one that I have read numerous times, and I am not at all surprised, looking at its heatmap, that its focus on topic 3 is broken near the end by the sudden dominance of topic 4. This change corresponds to a moment in Bigger's trial wherein the Jewish communist lawyer Boris Max gives an impassioned speech in defense of Bigger. In his speech, Max lays out the historical, social, and political conditions which have made Bigger who he is. It is not surprising that, in the chunks comprising this speech, the topic devoted to the discourse of race, slavery, and freedom is the most prominent.

From the cluster of novels steered by topic 4, I want to first address Killens's *Great Gittin' Up Morning*, since its publication date is way later than the other novels in the cluster. I had made the claim earlier that Killens's novel could potentially have an association with orality, as represented by topic 5. This claim is belied by the novel's heatmap; topic 5 appears in the novel, but with little presence. Instead, we have topic 4 as the most dominant topic, followed by topic 7. I had mentioned earlier that topic 4 was more interested in in the social and political dimensions of being enslaved and/or Black, whereas topic 7 focused on slavery as embodied experience. Killens differs from the rest of the cluster following topic 4 in [Figure 1](#Figure1) in its interest in enslaved embodiment. Topic 7 is a perceptible presence in *Clotel* and *Iola Leroy*—the two earliest novels in the corpus, both of which deal directly with slavery—but nowhere to the same extent as Killens's novel. Webb's *Garies* and Dunbar's *Sport* expectedly have topic 9, which represents the discourse of family, alongside topic 4 as a predominant topic. In Griggs's *Imperium*, which begins in a school, topic 1, to which the discourse of family is very relevant, accompanies the fourth topic. In Rogers's *"Superman"*, which is a didactic dialogue between an educated Black porter and a racist white politician, topic 4 seems to virtually stand alone. The unique prevalence of topic 7 in Killens's novel may have to do with its portrayal of a slave revolt. Such a depiction may require a heavy emphasis on the experience of being enslaved in addition to the social and political effects of slavery.

From the cluster aggregating around the intersection of the zero-lines for the X and Y axes, I want to choose one novel perceptibly moved by topics 6 and 10, and one novel really close to the center. Out of the first category, I pick Toni Morrison's *Love* (Morrison_Love_2003). Sitting at the coordinates of -0.14, -0.12 on [Figure 1](#Figure1), this novel decidedly shows some influence from topics 6 and 10, while still sticking close to the center. Looking at the heatmap for *Love*, we see a mélange of different topics. Topics 6 and 10 stand out the most, which is to be expected; they are followed by topics 9 and 1. In and of itself, the presence of these topics does not reveal much about Morrison's novel: it contains the discourses of family, the body, and domesticity, but so what? What is more interesting on *Love*'s heatmap is the fact that topic 6 takes up more than half of the penultimate chunk, and almost half of the last chunk. *Love* concerns seven women, whose lives are interwoven by their relationships to the deceased hotel owner Bill Cosey. The heading of the first eight chapters in this nine-chapter-long novel each evoke a different aspect of Cosey's social presence and status: "Portrait," "Friend," "Stranger," "Benefactor," "Lover," "Husband," "Guardian," and "Father." The last chapter upturhs this schema with its heading, "Phantom," which foregrounds Cosey's physical absence. It is ironic that "Phantom" corresponds to an increase in words pertinent to the discourse of the body. This could suggest that the matter of liberation from the all-suffusing phantom that is Cosey.

Out of the second category, I pick Alice Walker's *The Third Life of Grange Copeland* (AWalker_Copeland_1970), which is the closest novel to the zero point of [Figure 1](#Figure1). Much like *Love*. *Grange Copeland* is a novel that relates the intertwined lives of people. Specifically, Walker's novel concerns three generations of the Southern Black Copeland family from the 1920s to the 1960s. The patriarch of the family, Grange, is a poor sharecropper who is indebted to a white plantation owner, who is abusive toward his wife, and who has an affair with a prostitute, Josie. Eventually, Grange leaves his family and travels up North. Brownfield, Grange's son, follows in his father's footsteps: he engages in sexual activity with both Josie and her daughter; and he abuses his wife, Mem. When Grange comes back to the South, he develops a close bond with his granddaughter, Ruth, who is the focus of the second half of the book. Grange strives to create a better future for Ruth, who represents hope, much as Brownfield represents the repetition of the mistakes of past generations.

From this short summary, it can be inferred that sexuality is a dominant topic throughout the novel; accordingly, on the heatmap for Walker's novel, topic 6 appears as a consistent presence throughout most of the chunks. In the first half of the novel, topics 7 and 9 are predominant: topic 7, which pertains to the enslaved life, evokes the disadvantageous conditions under which Grange and Brownfield work, whereas topic 9 represents the family dynamics of the Copelands. Halfway through the heatmap, topic 9 (and, to a much lesser extent, topic 7) seems to lose its predominance, and topic 1, which foregrounds children and education, gains ground. This shift proceeds from the novel's shift of focus from Brownfield to Ruth. It is interesting that the discourse of family all but disappears in the latter half of the novel. This observation suggests that Grange's "third life" is his rejection of his previous duties as family patriarch in favor of his new goal of bettering Ruth's life.

## Conclusion

All in all, topic modeling presents a different way of looking at the patterns occurring in our corpus of 42 novels. The stylometric approach of the first three posts focused on the less conscious aspects of an author's writing that can nevertheless have its roots in the subject that the author writes about. In contrast, this post homed in directly on the thematic layers of the 42 novels.

This approach had its advantages and disadvantages. Someone reading this post may think that it's not terribly interesting that novels focusing on crime form a topic that focuses on crime: reading these novels would have just as well given us this information. I think, though, that automating this process of clustering novels together according to content has its uses, in that it allows us to see the discourses that produce novels, not to mention groupings—genres?—of novels, without having to read every single novel in the corpus. To be sure, once the results of such a computational method as topic modeling are obtained, there still needs to be some human reading done—it is optimal to use conventional and computational methods in tandem, instead of relying only on the latter.

Additionally, I think that my analysis of *Love* and *The Third Life of Grange Copeland* was particularly fruitful, in that it put the sinuous narrative trajectories of these two novels onto visual maps that showed thematic changes. This was so, despite the fact that these two novels belonged to the cluster that wasn't identified by one particular topic in [Figure 1](#Figure1). Digital humanist Ted Underwood[^6] notes that "meaningful ambiguity" is key to analyses done with topic modeling—if the results point to one straightforward theme, then the results are too evident upon cursory reading. The results yielded by applying topic modeling on Morrison's and Walker's novels do not do that, though; rather, they visualize, and aid in understanding, the complex structures of these novels.

All in all, topic modeling, a longstanding tool of computational literary analysis, proved useful in extracting thematic commonalities and idiosyncrasies in our small corpus of 42 Black novels. As with the stylometric analyses of the first three posts in this series, the results yielded by topic modeling requires the use of the faculties of reading and interpreting to make any sense. So, as I close this short series of computational analyses of the [*42 Books / 42 Years*](https://hbw.iu.edu/news-events/events/42Books-42Years/index.html) corpus, I invite you to mix computational and human modes of reading, also.
